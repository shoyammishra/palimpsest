Research brief · Project Palimpsest · revised 22 July 2026 with the first real-scale measurement and a three-pair reproducibility follow-up (F-016)

Can a trained model be rewritten instead of retrained?

Two models, same data, different training histories. What does it cost to turn one into the other — and when is that flatly impossible?

Optimization-path integrability Novelty case & evidence to date Written in plain language
IThe idea

Order leaves a permanent mark

Two cooks get identical ingredients and the same recipe. One adds the salt first and the acid last; the other does the reverse. The finished dishes are not the same — the order of the steps got cooked in. Now the harder question: given only the two finished dishes, can you turn one into the other with a small correction, without cooking either from scratch? And can you tell in advance how hard that will be — or that it is impossible, because some flavour can no longer be separated out?

Training a neural network is exactly that kind of ordered process. A model is not built in one shot. It is nudged, step by tiny step, by the data it sees, in the order it sees it, with a particular optimizer and schedule. Change the order and you get a different final model from the same data and the same compute. Everyone knows this much.

What nobody has answered is the question underneath it:

The question Given two models trained from the same ingredients but with different histories, what is the least computation needed to convert one into the other without retraining — and is there a number, measurable from the training path itself, that predicts that cost, including predicting when the cost is infinite?

That last clause matters. If some of the difference is permanently burned into the weights, no correction of the allowed kind will ever remove it, at any budget. We call such information path-burned.

Two outcomes are both real results. Either the law exists — training is, in a precise sense, partly rewritable — or it provably breaks past some point, which is an impossibility result. The project does not depend on the converter working.

IIWhat is new

The two nearest papers stop one step short — in opposite directions

This is the whole novelty case, and it is unusually clean. The closest published work on each side halts just before our question, and one of them names our branch as unexplored.

Sweeney · ICML 2026
Predicts which order is better
Computes a small quantity at the starting point and uses it to plan a good training order. It never looks at two finished models, never converts between them, and never discusses cost. Its own experiments show the local quantity loses accuracy over long training — 93% down to 65%.
The unoccupied middle · this project
Measures the cost of converting one finished model into another
Accumulate the order-sensitivity along the whole path, operate on finished models, and tie that accumulated number to conversion cost — with a hard limit where cost becomes infinite. Built so that both neighbours fall out as special cases.
Yu, He, Goyal & Arora · 2025
Proves a narrow impossibility
Shows that history-blind unlearning rules — the same fixed rule applied regardless of how each model was trained — push the two models further apart. The paper explicitly excludes history-aware methods, which is exactly what we build.

Their paper frames its result as “pick at most two of three” among: ignore the history, match the retrained model, use only local rules. They give up matching. We give up ignoring the history — our method is told the history difference and uses it. The authors state that branch exists and do not pursue it. Our central question is literally how much budget makes it affordable.

The four things that are new together

  • § We accumulate along the whole path, not at one point. Prior order-sensitivity work computes a local number at the start and uses it to plan. We add it up along the entire run, which is what turns a planning heuristic into a property of a finished model.
  • § We convert between finished models. No prior work takes two already-trained models with different histories and edits one toward the other without replaying the data. We built one that works.
  • § We propose a cost law, not a gadget. A quantitative relationship between a measured property of the training path and the compute needed to convert. Laws generalize; editing tricks do not.
  • § Our impossibility floor contains theirs as a special case. We proved a limit for any method confined to a fixed set of directions. Set those directions to the ones history-blind rules are stuck in, and the published 2025 theorem falls out. An incremental paper cannot swallow a competitor's theorem; ours is built to.

“Adds up along the whole path” means the quantity accumulates over training rather than being computed at a single point. “Hard limit” means the work identifies when conversion is impossible at any budget. The last cell is the honest one: scale-up is the declared next step, using the public PolyPythias runs rather than pretraining from scratch. That path is currently partially degraded, not clean: our own audit of the PolyPythias 160M checkpoints — run before drawing any conclusion — found the init-only control arm void beyond step 0 (all four decoupled runs numerically identical from step 1), so a replacement control is a pending decision; the cause is upstream, not yet determined, and reported to the maintainers. The order-only arm survives and has now delivered the measurement in Section IV.

Fields we sit next to, and are not

  • Machine unlearning removes specific data. That is one special case of converting any history into another.
  • Model editing (ROME, MEMIT) changes specific facts. It targets facts; we target histories, and ask about cost and limits rather than a single recipe.
  • Model merging and task arithmetic combine endpoints heuristically. They never ask what conversion costs or when it is impossible.
  • Linear mode connectivity studies the geometry between two endpoints, not the cost of moving between them using the history.
  • Influence functions measure one data point's effect — a per-point sensitivity, not conversion between full histories.
  • Curriculum learning chooses a good order up front; training-order recovery infers which order was used. Both are “what was the order”, not “can the result be rewritten, and at what cost”.
  • The NTK / lazy regime is where order genuinely does not matter — so any positive result must be shown outside it, or it is folklore. That is a discipline we impose on ourselves.
IVEvidence so far

What is proved, what is measured, and at what scale

Proved on paper

Momentum makes order matter more — by a predictable amount. With plain gradient descent, order effects shrink quadratically as you take smaller steps: halve the step, the effect drops to a quarter. We proved that with momentum — the running memory of recent updates that nearly every modern optimizer keeps — the effect shrinks only linearly. Halve the step, halve the effect. The memory of order lives in the optimizer's own state and leaks into the model over time. A separate 2026 paper reported this empirically; we derive it, and pin down its size.

An impossibility floor that swallows a published theorem. For any method allowed to move the model only within a fixed set of directions — imagine being able to push north–south but never east–west — part of the difference between two models can never be removed, at any budget. The right measure of that permanent residual is subtle: it is a projection distance, because allowed motion can partly compensate for forbidden directions, so the naive answer is wrong. Honest limit: this holds for fixed-direction methods. Real fine-tuning can change its reachable directions as it goes, and extending the floor to that case is open.

The key quantity is measurable from one run. The number that predicts the conversion gap can be computed from a single model's saved checkpoints. No retraining of intermediate versions. That is what makes this an instrument rather than a thought experiment.

Measured in code — on a few-hundred-parameter network

Every number below comes from a deliberately tiny network in high-precision arithmetic with fixed seeds, run as a cheap smoke test of the exact pipeline before any expensive run. None of these are large-scale claims.

The obvious next shortcut provably fails here — and we know why. “Sample a few corrections instead of computing all of them” was pre-registered as a predicted failure. 600 / 982
The breakdown point is a frontier, not a wall. Overshoot in raw-weight terms grows smoothly with severity and crosses over at about 600 of 982 swaps at our step size — and halving the step size roughly halves the overshoot. So the approximation is asymptotically exact for small steps. Behavioural quality keeps improving well past the raw-weight edge.
refuted
We killed our own formula, on our own rules. A candidate closed-form law for that frontier fit every existing cell within ~12%. We froze it, pre-registered exact pass/fail thresholds, and re-tested on new configurations. It failed twice at the margin — one prediction off by 25.4% against a ±25% gate, and R² = 0.9791 against a 0.98 gate. We count it refuted and do not use it, even though it out-predicted both rival formulas 4–5×. The failed test paid for itself: it revealed the frontier's constant depends strongly on run length — shrinking about 3.4× when the run doubles — which the formula missed entirely. A rescaled version (severity as a fraction of maximal disorder) reconciles the numbers to 18% — and is now a flagged observation awaiting its own pre-registered test. One more structural fact: which swaps occur, not just how many, changes the error by about 30% at matched severity — a lead for the theory, and a warning against one-number severity scales.

Measured at real scale — 160 million parameters, 300 billion tokens

The results above are the smoke test. In July 2026 we ran the first real-scale measurement. It is a measurement, not a conversion: it asks how much of the difference between two large trained models is due to training order alone. We took two public Pythia-160M language models trained on the same 300 billion tokens from the identical starting point, differing only in the order the data arrived — the cleanest same-ingredients/different-history pair that exists publicly — plus a reference pair differing in both the starting point and the order. At twelve snapshots spanning training we measured how differently each pair predicts the next word, over 98,304 evaluated positions. Thirty-six cells, none void, every hypothesis and threshold committed to the repository before a single checkpoint was downloaded.

A number like this is easy to fool yourself with, so we bracketed it before interpreting it. The floor: how different are two snapshots of the same run, a thousand steps apart? Below that is noise, not history. The ceiling: how different is a 160M model from a 410M one? At or above that would mean reordering the data is as big as changing the model.

Order alone clears the noise floor. At the end of training the order-only pair measures 0.290461 against a floor of 0.076275. Data order by itself leaves a behavioural difference comfortably larger than the run's own step-to-step wobble. pre-registered · passed
0.46 ceiling
And stays under the ceiling. The 160M-vs-410M size gap measures 0.460360, so the order-only difference is about 63% of a 2.5×-model-size difference — large, but not as large as changing the model. pre-registered · passed
≈ 0.95
Late in training, order alone accounts for roughly 95% of what order plus a different starting point produce together. The order-only share of the joint difference rises steadily: ≈0 at the earliest snapshots, 0.319 at step 256, 0.546 at 1,000, 0.857 at 4,000, 0.932 at 16,000, then between 0.943 and 0.993 across the last three snapshots, ending at 0.949. The rise is not perfectly monotone — the final step ticks down from 0.993 to 0.949. Read plainly: by mid-training, giving the second model an entirely different random starting point on top of the reordered data buys almost no extra difference in behaviour. The starting point's fingerprint washes out; order's does not.
behaviour, not weights
The models do not converge internally. In raw-weight terms the pairs never approach each other — the order-only pair's weight distance grows to 0.954 (peaking at 0.966 mid-training), and the joint pair stays at full independent-starting-point scale throughout (1.247 → 1.408). What converges is what the models do. That is the regime the project declared in advance as the claim-bearing one.
exact
The instrument certified itself. A pre-declared reproducibility cell re-ran one measurement from scratch and reproduced it exactly, and a tripwire written in advance to catch the checkpoint defect described in Section III stayed clean across the whole grid. confirmed
Caveats that travel with these numbers One measurement, with real limits — quote the number with these or not at all.
  • Originally one pair per arm; the order-only arm is now three, the joint arm still one. The order-only measurement has since been reproduced across three correlated pairs agreeing to ~1% (see the follow-up below), so “the order-only share is ~0.95” is no longer plausibly a single-seed artifact — but there are still no error bars on the joint pair, and the three order-only pairs share one starting point, so run-to-run starting-point variation is unmeasured.
  • Late training only. The noise floor is strongly time-dependent: adjacent-snapshot drift is 0.680 early (steps 1,000→2,000), 0.141 mid-training, 0.076 at the end. The early floor exceeds every pair value at every point on the curve, so snapshots before roughly step 1,000 support no claim about the pairs at all.
  • We could not measure the starting point's own contribution. No usable public control exists for “same order, different starting point” — the candidate failed our own audit (Section III). So the joint-minus-order-only difference mixes the starting point's own effect with its interaction with order, and the prediction about when each accumulates is untested — neither confirmed nor refuted.
  • The gate we passed is weaker than the one we registered. Losing that control arm forced us to re-register the joint-dominance test against one surviving comparison instead of two. We passed the weaker version, and say so.
  • The evaluation text is from the training distribution, not held out. Registered in advance as acceptable because the measurement compares two models against each other rather than testing memorization — but nothing here speaks to generalization.
  • For scale, two completely untrained models already differ by 0.3995 on this measure — the same order of magnitude as the end-of-training values (0.29–0.31). The measure has a naturally large baseline, which is precisely why the floor and ceiling brackets are load-bearing rather than decorative.
  • One oddity we do not explain. The joint pair's curve is non-monotone: 0.400 untrained, dipping to 0.186 at step 256 as both runs learn the same basic statistics of language, then recovering to about 0.31. Recorded, not understood.

A follow-up narrows the first caveat (22 July 2026, F-016). Because “one pair per arm” is the most serious limitation, we spent the cheapest available information against it. Reusing the identical instrument — same snapshots, evaluation text, measure and pre-registered thresholds — we added two more order-only pairs by bringing in a third public 160M run trained on the same 300B tokens from the same starting point in another data order, giving three order-only pairs where before there was one. Their end-of-training order-induced differences agree closely — 0.290461, 0.290638, 0.293055, a spread of about 1% — and the ~0.95 late-training saturation reproduces across all three (0.949 / 0.949 / 0.957 of the joint difference). So the order-only effect is reproducible, not a lucky pair of seeds, and the ~5% of the joint difference that order does not account for reads as a structural residual (a durable trace of the starting point, and/or an interaction between starting point and order) rather than single-pair noise. Three honesty notes travel with it: the three pairs are correlated (three pairings among three runs sharing one starting point), so this bounds order-only pair-to-pair noise only and says nothing about starting-point-to-starting-point variation; the joint reference is still a single pair; and the ~1% tightness is an observation, not a gated claim — the pre-registered gate only required the spread to stay within a factor of two, and it passed at ~1%. It does not create the missing “different starting point, same order” control, so it still cannot separate “durable trace” from “interaction”.

Relation to prior work — this confirms and extends; it does not discover. That the earliest phase of training decides run-to-run variation, and that networks become insensitive to their own starting conditions after a short prefix, is established: it is the substance of Frankle, Dziugaite, Roy and Carbin's instability analysis (2020) and the early-phase-of-training literature, and the PolyPythias suite we used was itself built to study seed stability. Our contribution here is narrower and should be stated narrowly — a behaviour-space decomposition, at 160M parameters and 300B tokens, of how much run-to-run difference is due to order alone across the whole of training.

What this is not. It is not a conversion, and not a cost-law data point at scale. Our converter has not been run at 160M parameters. This establishes that the thing we propose to convert — an order-induced difference — is real, sizeable and durable at real scale. Whether it can be converted away, and at what cost, is still a pilot-scale result only, and is the next open question.

The cost caveat, reported rather than buried

The converter currently costs 3.8× to 38.6× retraining under our declared accounting rules, down from 25×–713× at first build. At this scale it beats retraining on cost for nothing, and we say so plainly. Of the two efficiency ideas declared before any cost claim, one worked exactly as hoped and the other was honestly closed by the pre-registered negative result above. What remains open is structural: keep-the-largest-corrections estimators, and a measured trend suggesting sampling gets easier as histories diverge further — a scale question, flagged rather than claimed.

Framing discipline Possible is not the same as cheap. A cost that exceeds retraining is a measurement of the phase boundary, not something to hide. And an impossibility result is a first-class outcome, not a failure.
VWhy it is broad

This is not a paper about data ordering

The fair worry is that shuffling data is a niche. Data order is one coordinate of a much larger object — the optimization history — and the framework is generic in three independent ways.

Broad in what counts as history

The same machinery covers curriculum design, choice of optimizer, learning-rate schedules, data replay, augmentation schedules, and the staged pipelines behind instruction-tuning, RLHF, and alignment. Any two runs that share ingredients but differ in how they were combined are a valid pair for our question.

Broad in optimizer and architecture

Nothing is tied to a network shape. One deliverable is precisely that results stratify by optimizer class: which training choices leave history correctable, and which burn it in permanently. The plain → momentum → Adam chain is the first proved instance, now numerically confirmed end to end.

Broad in what it is good for

  • Unlearning and its regulatory verification. When a law requires that a user's data be deleted, is the influence actually erasable — or path-burned? The impossibility floor is a direct tool for answering “no method of this class can remove this, at any budget.”
  • Reproducibility. How much does run-to-run history variation change behaviour, and can it be corrected after the fact instead of by re-running?
  • Merging and editing. Why do some merges work and others fail? A cost-and-limit law over histories explains which combinations are reachable.
  • Continual learning. When tasks arrive in sequence, what is permanently burned in versus still correctable?
  • Training as an audit trail. What does a finished model reveal — or provably fail to reveal — about how it was made?
VIRisks

What could go wrong, and what we are doing about it

Reviewers call it incremental

Our own novelty audit flagged this as the single largest risk for the original framing. We fixed it structurally, not cosmetically, by rebuilding the core around the cost-and-limit law — the one thing that provably subsumes the nearest prior works as special cases.

The question is secretly already answered

Estimated low but nonzero, and treated as a hard gate before spending any compute: we read both gating papers end to end, including appendices, and logged exactly what each does and does not do. We re-run a targeted preprint check before the large-scale phase, since a fast follow-up could close the gap.

Hidden overlap we have not found

Real and nonzero. Mitigated by the end-to-end reads, an explicit map of adjacent fields, and preemptive citation of the earlier local-quantity work so the lineage is transparent rather than discovered by a reviewer.

The conversion results are all tiny-scale

Partly true, and now precisely bounded. Every conversion result is on a few-hundred-parameter network; the first measurement result is at 160M parameters and 300B tokens (Section IV). The tiny scale was deliberate: cheapest-first smoke testing of the exact pipeline, and the scale-up path uses the public PolyPythias runs so we do not pretrain from scratch. Honest status: that path is partially degraded, not clean — an audit of the PolyPythias 160M checkpoints (our own, before any conclusion was drawn) found the init-only control arm void beyond step 0, so a replacement control is pending; the order-only arm survives and has now been run end to end, and the anomaly was caught by gate design rather than propagating into a claim. Running the converter at 160M remains the open scale-up step.

The real-scale measurement rests on too few model pairs

Partly addressed, and honestly bounded. The obvious next spend was taken for the order-only arm: it has since been reproduced across three correlated pairs agreeing to ~1% (F-016), so “order alone accounts for ~95% of joint divergence late in training” is no longer separable-from-a-single-seed-artifact for the order-only effect. Still live: the joint arm is a single pair (no error bars on it), the three order-only pairs share one starting point (so starting-point-to-starting-point variation is unmeasured), and the ~1% agreement is an observation rather than a gated claim. The brackets that make the numbers interpretable — a same-run noise floor and a cross-model-size ceiling — were fixed in advance; additional joint-arm and different-starting-point seed pairs are the next spend. Equally live: the separation of scales holds only late in training, because the noise floor early in training exceeds every pair value we measured.

No init-only control exists, so one arm of the design is unavailable

True — and a limitation of the public artifacts rather than of our method. The only public candidate for a “same order, different starting point” pair failed our own continuity audit, and no replacement exists in the open. The consequence is stated wherever the numbers appear: the joint-minus-order-only difference confounds the starting point's own effect with its interaction with order, and the prediction about when order- versus init-differences accumulate is untested at real scale — neither confirmed nor refuted. The follow-up above (F-016) sharpens why this control matters: having shown the ~5% residual is structural rather than noise, an init-only control is exactly what would separate “durable starting-point trace” from “starting-point × order interaction”. We have asked the one group known to hold suitable weights; if they do not materialize, training a small matched pair ourselves is the fallback, priced and deferred.

The converter is more expensive than retraining

True and reported openly. The remaining levers are named, the fixed overhead shrinks relative to retraining as models grow, and even if conversion stays expensive, the cost curve is the phase-boundary measurement — which is itself the contribution.

The impossibility floor covers only a restricted method class

True. Extending it from fixed-direction methods to methods that adapt their reachable directions is open. It is stated as open — and the restricted case already contains a published theorem as a special case, which stands on its own.

Why this fits a top-tier venue

A candidate new quantitative law rather than a new gadget; impossibility results as a first-class outcome, which NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR actively reward; and an optimizer classification tied to reversibility, with the first instance already proved and numerically confirmed. The area is demonstrably live — our two nearest neighbours are an ICML 2026 paper and a 2025 theory paper — and we occupy a branch that one of them names as unexplored.
